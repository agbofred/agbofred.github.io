Introducing the Conversational Paradigm

PART ONE

Conversation is becoming a key mode of human-computer interaction — what does that actually mean?

From Commands to Conversation

A brief callback to where this interaction paradigm came from.

Command lines demanded formal syntax; graphical interfaces let people manipulate visible objects directly; conversational agents now ask people to simply say what they want, in their own words — shifting the burden of translation back onto the machine.

A key mode of human-computer interaction: by 2016, researchers Ewa Luger and Abigail Sellen observed that nearly every major technology company — Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Facebook — had shipped its own conversational agent, and dialogue itself was quickly becoming a mainstream way of interacting with computers.

Luger, E., & Sellen, A. (2016). “Like Having a Really Bad PA.” CHI ’16.

What Is a Conversational Agent?

An interface where natural language dialogue — not visual manipulation — is the primary means of interaction.

Text-Based

Typed or written natural language, exchanged in a chat-style thread.

Voice-Based

Spoken language, recognized and synthesized in real time.

Multimodal

Conversation combined with visual, gestural, or touch input and output.

All three share one defining trait: the interface asks you to say what you want, instead of showing you where to click.

What Distinguishes a Conversational Interface from a GUI?

The Interaction Design Foundation names three interface formats — each asks something very different of the user.

Graphical (GUI)

Users interact with visual representations on digital control panels — a computer’s desktop is a GUI.

Voice-Controlled (VUI)

Users interact through their voices — most smart assistants, e.g. Siri and Alexa, are VUIs.

Gesture-Based

Users engage with 3D design spaces through bodily motions — e.g. in virtual reality.

Interaction Design Foundation, What is User Interface (UI) Design?

The GUI: Visible, Direct, Persistent

Recall Shneiderman’s direct manipulation principles from earlier in the course.

A GUI shows you the objects of interest continuously: files, buttons, menus stay on screen, visible and clickable, until you act on them.

  • Visibility — the state of the system and its options are always on screen
  • Direct manipulation — actions are physical: point, click, drag
  • Persistence — the interface doesn’t disappear after each action

The CUI: Sequential, Implicit, Ephemeral

A conversational interface trades all three GUI properties for something much less forgiving.

Sequential

No menu of options on screen — the user must generate the right words, in order, from memory.

Implicit

System state and capability aren’t shown; they must be inferred or guessed at.

Ephemeral

Once a turn passes, it’s gone — nothing to point back to.

GUI vs. Conversational Interface, Side by Side

The same underlying task, framed through two very different interaction models.

Graphical Interface (GUI) Conversational Interface (CUI)
Discoverability Options are visible Options are invisible — you must guess
Error recovery Undo, back button, visible history Rephrase, repeat, or start over
Learning curve Recognition — browse menus Recall — remember or infer phrasing
State Persistent on screen Held in short-term memory, if at all
Feedback Immediate, visual Sequential, often just words

The Hidden Complexity: Turn-Taking & Grounding

Human conversation runs on machinery most of us never notice we’re using.

Herbert Clark and Susan Brennan’s influential 1991 account describes conversation as a joint activity: participants build “common ground” — shared knowledge, beliefs, and assumptions — through a moment-by-moment process called grounding.

The grounding criterion: a speaker and listener mutually believe the listener has understood “to a criterion sufficient for current purposes.” In human talk, this happens automatically through nods, “mm-hm,” and eye contact. A conversational agent has to approximate all of that using text or audio alone.

Clark, H. H., & Brennan, S. E. (1991). Grounding in Communication. APA.

Why Grounding Is Hard for Machines

The cues humans use to ground a conversation mostly aren’t available to a CA.

  • No nonverbal cues — nods, facial expressions, and eye contact carry huge amounts of grounding information in face-to-face talk; text and most voice interfaces have none of it.
  • Least collaborative effort, reversed — a CA can’t always tell when a short reply signals confusion instead of confirmation.
  • No shared physical context — human listeners ground references using shared surroundings (“the blue one”); most agents have no access to what the user can see.

The Design Space of Conversational Agents

PART TWO

Text-based chatbots, voice assistants, and multimodal agents each make different trade-offs.

Text-Based Chatbots

The most constrained — and most forgiving — point in the design space.

What makes it distinct

  • Persistent transcript the user can scroll back through
  • No speech recognition errors to contend with
  • Supports rich text, links, buttons, images inline
  • Slower-paced than speech, giving users time to think

Where text still beats speech

The transcript partially restores the persistence a GUI offers — a user can scroll up, copy information, or re-read a complex answer.

Examples: customer support widgets, SMS services, Slack/Teams bots.

Voice Assistants (VUIs)

No screen, no transcript, no visible history — the most demanding point in the space.

What makes it distinct

  • Entirely hands-free and eyes-free
  • Speech recognition errors compound with every misheard word
  • Nothing persists once spoken
  • Fastest, most natural-feeling input for comfortable speakers

The cost of losing the screen

Every GUI convenience disappears. The user must hold everything in working memory — what they asked, what was said, what to say next.

Examples: Amazon Alexa, Google Assistant (audio-only), Siri via HomePod.

Multimodal Agents

Conversation plus a screen — borrowing GUI persistence back for a CUI.

What makes it distinct

  • Combines speech or text with visual output
  • Lets the agent show a result instead of describing it
  • Users can switch modes mid-task
  • Reintroduces GUI-style visibility without abandoning language

The best of both, if done well

A multimodal agent can answer with both a spoken summary and a visual result — ephemeral warmth plus persistent clarity, together.

Examples: Echo Show, Nest Hub, ChatGPT with canvas output.

Comparing the Three Types

No type is strictly better — each trades away something the others keep.

Text Chatbot Voice Assistant Multimodal
Persistence High (scrollback) None High (screen)
Hands/eyes-free No Yes Partial
Error tolerance High Low Medium
Richness of output Text, links, images Audio only Full multimedia

The Expectations Users Bring

PART THREE

A landmark study asks: what do people actually think a conversational agent can do?

“Like Having a Really Bad PA”

Luger & Sellen (2016) — a landmark CHI study of everyday conversational agent use.

By the mid-2010s, every major tech company had shipped its own conversational agent — yet almost no research had looked at how people actually used these systems day to day, outside the lab. Luger and Sellen set out to close that gap.

14 semi-structured interviews with existing CA users
2 guiding questions: what motivates, and what limits, everyday use
1 core theoretical lens: Norman’s “gulfs of execution and evaluation”

Luger, E., & Sellen, A. (2016). CHI ’16, 5286–5297.

Norman’s Gulfs, Applied to Conversation

Luger & Sellen use Don Norman’s classic HCI framework to interpret their findings.

The Gulf of Execution

The distance between what a user wants to do and what they must actually say to make it happen. A GUI narrows this with visible buttons; a CA leaves the user guessing.

The Gulf of Evaluation

The distance between what the system did and what the user can tell it did. Without a screen, users often can’t tell if they were misheard or misunderstood.

The Central Finding

User expectations were dramatically out of step with how the systems actually operate.

Luger and Sellen found that users’ beliefs about their conversational agents were dramatically out of step with the operation of the systems themselves — particularly around three things: known machine intelligence, system capability, and system goals.

  • Machine intelligence — how smart the agent actually is, versus how smart it seems
  • System capability — what tasks it can complete, versus what users assume
  • System goals — what the agent optimizes for, rarely visible to the user

What “Like Having a Really Bad PA” Reveals

The paper’s title is itself a finding — users reach for a human metaphor, then feel betrayed by it.

Users described their conversational agents using a human role — a Personal Assistant — because that’s the closest existing mental model available. But a PA understands context, remembers preferences, and asks smart clarifying questions. When the CA falls short, the comparison itself produces the disappointment.

The design lesson: the metaphor a product invites — “assistant,” “friend,” “helper” — sets the bar the system will be judged against, whether or not that bar was intentional.

Design Implications: Narrowing the Gulf

What follows from taking Luger & Sellen’s findings seriously.

  • Signal what’s actually possible — narrow the gulf of execution with example prompts, not a blank text box
  • Make outcomes visible — narrow the gulf of evaluation with explicit confirmations, not silence
  • Calibrate the intelligence claim — avoid interface choices that imply more understanding than the system has
  • Design for repair, not just success — assume misunderstanding will happen, and make it cheap to correct

Choosing the Right Paradigm

A conversational agent is a design choice, not a default.

  • Prefer a GUI when… users need to compare many options, browse freely, or the task benefits from persistent, visible state
  • Prefer a CUI when… the task is narrow and well-defined, hands/eyes are busy, or natural language genuinely simplifies the request
  • Prefer multimodal when… the task has both — a quick spoken request with a result best shown, not described

Key Takeaways

What separates a conversational agent from every interface this course has studied before.

  • A conversational interface asks users to generate the right words from memory, instead of recognizing options on screen — trading GUI visibility, persistence, and direct manipulation for natural language.
  • Conversation depends on grounding — Clark & Brennan’s term for confirming mutual understanding. Machines have far fewer grounding cues than humans.
  • Text chatbots, voice assistants, and multimodal agents trade off persistence, hands-free use, error tolerance, and output richness — none is universally best.
  • Luger & Sellen show user expectations are often dramatically out of step with what CAs actually do — and the human metaphors we invite are a large part of why.
