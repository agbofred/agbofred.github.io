Humans Across the History of AI

PART ONE

Every era of AI has redefined what the human in the system is actually for.

Era 1 · Symbolic AI & Expert Systems

1950s – late 1980s · The human as knowledge engineer

Experts hand-coded rules, facts, and heuristics into systems like MYCIN and DENDRAL. The machine could only reason about what a person had explicitly told it.

  • Authorship — people wrote IF–THEN rules by hand; no learning from data
  • Bottleneck — progress capped by how fast experts could articulate what they knew
  • Brittleness — systems failed outside the exact scenarios their rules anticipated

Human input = the entire knowledge base.

Era 2 · Statistical & Machine Learning AI

1990s – early 2010s · The human as data labeler & feature designer

Systems learned statistical patterns from examples. People shifted from writing rules to curating labeled data and hand-crafting the features models learned from.

  • Supervision — humans supplied ground-truth labels defining “correct”
  • Feature engineering — experts decided which signals were worth learning from
  • Emerging oversight roles — monitoring and correcting deployed models

Human input = labeled examples + engineered features.

Era 3 · Deep Learning, Foundation Models & LLMs

2012 – present · The human as trainer, evaluator & prompt-writer

Large pretrained models shifted human effort from building capability to shaping and steering it — through preference data, evaluation, and natural-language prompting.

  • RLHF & preference data — annotators rank outputs; judgments become training signal
  • Prompting as interaction — everyday users “program” behavior in natural language
  • Evaluation & red-teaming — humans probe for failures automated metrics miss

Human input = preferences, feedback, and instructions in natural language.

The throughline: from author to collaborator

As machines took on more of the reasoning, the human role moved up a level of abstraction each time.

Levels of automation

Sheridan & Verplank’s (1978) scale — later adapted by Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens (2000) — breaks “automated or not” into a graded scale of who decides and who acts.

Level Description
1 — Human does it all No AI assistance; the person decides and acts
3 — AI narrows the options The system narrows alternatives down to a few for the human to pick from
5 — AI recommends & waits for approval The system picks an action but executes only if approved
7 — AI acts, then informs The system executes automatically and tells the human afterward
10 — Full automation The system decides, acts, and reports to no one

Why oversight matters

  • Accountability — someone must be answerable when an AI decision causes harm; oversight determines who that is and when they had a chance to act
  • Error correction — humans catch failure modes the model wasn’t trained to recognize, especially in rare or novel situations
  • Trust calibration — well-placed oversight prevents both over-trust (complacency) and under-trust (needless double-checking)
  • Skill retention — too little involvement over time can erode the human expertise needed to catch AI mistakes at all

Human-AI Collaboration & Teaming

PART THREE

Beyond oversight: what does it take for a person and an AI system to genuinely work as a team?

Principles of human-AI teaming

Informed by Klein et al.’s (2004) “Ten Challenges for Making Automation a Team Player” and later human-automation teaming research.

Mutual predictability Each teammate — human or AI — can reasonably anticipate what the other will do next.

Shared mental models Both sides hold a compatible understanding of the goal, the state of the task, and each other’s roles.

Complementarity The team is designed around what each party does best, not around forcing one to imitate the other.

Graceful degradation When one teammate fails or is uncertain, the team as a whole should fail safely, not catastrophically.

What is interactive machine learning?

Amershi et al. (2014) define IML by how its update cycle differs from the traditional applied ML workflow.

Traditional Applied ML Interactive Machine Learning
Long, asynchronous iterations mediated by ML practitioners Rapid — the model updates immediately in response to user input
End users give data/feedback only indirectly, through the practitioner Focused — only a particular aspect of the model changes each time
Model updates are infrequent and can change the model drastically Incremental — each update is small, so users can steer via trial and error

Discussion questions for the reading

Power to the People: The Role of Humans in Interactive Machine Learning (Amershi et al., 2014)

  1. The paper argues user and learning system are coupled so tightly it’s difficult to study one in isolation. Does this complicate the in-/on-/out-of-the-loop spectrum from Part 2?
  2. Cakmak et al. found users resented being treated as constant-query “oracles.” What does this suggest about designing for oversight versus what users actually want?
  3. People give systematically more positive than negative feedback when teaching agents. How does that stress-test “mutual predictability” and “shared mental models” from Part 3?
  4. Compare Crayons and Wekinator to the three collaboration patterns from Part 3. Which pattern best fits each system, and why?
  5. The authors call for closer HCI–ML collaboration. Where in the Part 1 history do you see evidence this collaboration was missing?

Key takeaways

  • The human’s role in AI hasn’t disappeared as systems got smarter — it has moved up a level, from encoding knowledge to steering and evaluating it.
  • “Human-in-the-loop” is not one thing; it’s a design choice along a spectrum of control, monitoring, and accountability.
  • Good collaboration requires more than a capable AI — it requires mutual predictability, shared understanding, and graceful failure.

Discussion: As a system moves from in-the-loop to out-of-the-loop, what design elements should travel with it to preserve accountability? Pick a tool you use today — which era’s human role does it mostly still resemble?
